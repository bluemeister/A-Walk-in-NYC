Somewhere to Sit

Open maps on your phone in a city you know well. Zoom in to approximately a 10 by 10 block radius. Take a screenshot. Draw a virtual line across your screenshot. Walk along the line as best you can. Notice all the deviations you must take.

Field notes
The map is a piece of the Manhattan grid, rotated so the avenues run straight up, over the City of New York's own 2018 aerial photography (maps.nyc.gov, CC BY 4.0). Each game opens a different piece of the grid. The dots are the privately owned public spaces (POPS) in NYC Planning's public dataset: plazas, arcades and atriums that developers built in exchange for the right to build taller. There are 366 of them in Manhattan. Whether a space has seating, whether it is indoors, and whether its hours are restricted come from that dataset. What happens when you arrive is decided by those three facts and by the time on the clock.

You draw the shape of the trip you intend, but the length that trip must cover is set by the round's difficulty, not by you — draw something shorter or longer than the required range and it is rejected before it starts. That closes the obvious loophole: you cannot inflate your score by giving yourself an easy, trivial swipe. The lit corners are the only steps you can take from where you stand — you choose the actual path, block by block, and it is scored against the shortest path the grid could have given you: the blue dotted line. Every so often your legs give out, and the only places to rest belong to someone. Score well against that fixed difficulty and the next round tightens — longer required trips, shorter legs, earlier closings. Score badly and it loosens. Each choice is logged, the way a phone would log it, and your record accumulates below.
